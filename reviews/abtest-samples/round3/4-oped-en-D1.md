# I'd skip the Wi-Fi on an air fryer and pay for it on a sous-vide cooker

By Dana Whitfield, head of product at Halden Kitchen

The Wi-Fi version of an air fryer at Halden typically costs $40 to $60 more than the same model without it. I've been head of product here for nine years, so it may seem odd for me to tell you that most people shouldn't pay that. But I've read what our customers did after they bought one, and for most smart appliances my answer to "is it worth the extra money?" is no. There's one exception, and it explains the rest.

In 2025 we surveyed 1,200 buyers of connected appliances. Only 61% had connected the app even once, which means about four in ten had paid for a feature and never switched it on. Three months after purchase, 18% were still using the app. Out of 1,200 people, that's roughly 216.

Those numbers tell me how many people stopped and not why. My guess is that once the novelty wears off, walking over to the machine is easier than finding the app, but that's only a guess. What we do know is what the app was mostly used for, and the most common answer was a notification that cooking is done. If that were written honestly on the box, it would read "texts you when the chicken's ready, $50," and I don't think many people would reach for their wallets.

If that were the whole cost, I might shrug and call it a harmless extra. It isn't, because connected models came back to us 1.4 times as often as their non-connected versions, and the top reason buyers gave was "the app wouldn't pair." Think about what that means for the person at home. They paid more for a feature, spent some part of an evening failing to get it working, and then packed up the whole appliance and sent it back, when the thing may well have cooked perfectly. On the cheaper version of the same model, that particular evening can't happen, since there's nothing to pair.

Now the exception. Among buyers of connected sous-vide cookers, 54% were still using the app after three months, three times the overall figure. I don't read that as sous-vide people being more devoted to gadgets. I read it as the app finally having a job to do. A sous-vide cook takes hours, and over hours you'll probably want to leave the kitchen, and possibly the house. Say you've got something in the bath all afternoon and you're out running errands. You can check on it and adjust it from your phone, which no button on the machine can do for you from across town.

An air fryer doesn't run for hours. By the time you'd think to check on it, you could have walked over and looked. So the app ends up doing the one thing left for it, which is telling you the food is ready.

That gives you a simple test for any appliance with a Wi-Fi version, and it has nothing to do with how clever the app looks in the product photos. Ask whether this machine will ever be cooking long enough that you'd want to check on it or change something from another room or another building. If yes, the connected version may well earn its price, and the sous-vide numbers suggest it does. If the best use you can come up with is a heads-up that dinner's done, buy the cheaper one.

We still sell both versions of most models, and I'm not trying to talk anyone out of a choice they've thought through. That 18% is real, and if you already know you're the sort of person who'll still be opening the app in three months, enjoy it. Everyone else shopping for an air fryer should keep the $40 to $60 and spend it on something they'll still be using by then.
