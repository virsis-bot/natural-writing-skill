# I'd skip the Wi-Fi on an air fryer and pay for it on a sous-vide cooker

*By Dana Whitfield, head of product at Halden Kitchen*

Are smart kitchen appliances worth paying extra for? Mostly no, and I'm saying that after nine years as head of product at a shop that sells them and charges more for them. There is one clear exception, and it tells you what to look for before you buy anything with an app.

Take the air fryer, because the price is easy to see. At Halden, the Wi-Fi version typically costs $40–60 more than the same model without it. That would be fair if people used what they paid for, so in 2025 we surveyed 1,200 buyers of connected appliances after their purchase and asked.

61% had connected the app at least once, which means about four in ten never connected it at all. Three months after buying, 18% were still using it. Out of 1,200 people, that's roughly 216. Everyone else had paid for a feature they'd dropped or had never switched on.

And what did people use the app for? Connected cooking sounds like it should involve something clever, but the most common use was a notification that cooking is done. Set that next to the air fryer's price tag and you have $40–60 for a buzz in your pocket.

An app you ignore costs you the money and a little embarrassment. The returns are worse. Connected models came back 1.4 times as often as their non-connected versions, and the top reason was "the app wouldn't pair." Think about what that means for the person sending it back. They wanted an air fryer, they paid extra for a feature they had maybe a one-in-five chance of still using by the end of the third month, and that feature is why they're now standing in the hall with a box and no air fryer. The plain model can't fail that way, since it has nothing to pair.

If that were all the survey said, this column would be a flat no. Then we looked at sous-vide cookers. 54% of people who bought a connected one were still using the app after three months, three times the 18% for connected appliances as a whole.

The reason isn't complicated. A sous-vide cook takes hours, so you're going to wander off, and the food will sit in its water bath while you're in another room or out of the house altogether. Being able to check on it and adjust it from your phone is useful there in a way a done-cooking alert on an air fryer isn't. I don't want to oversell this, because 54% still leaves plenty of people who gave up on the app. But it's the one result in our survey where a connected feature held on to most of its buyers.

My reading, and the survey doesn't prove it, is that an app earns its price when two things are true: the appliance runs long enough that you'll leave it, and there's something you'd want to do about it from wherever you've gone. An air fryer fails on both counts for most people. You're close by, and the only news your phone has for you is that the food is ready.

So when you're looking at a connected version of anything, ask how long this thing runs and what you'd actually do from your phone while it does. If your honest answer to the second part is "find out it's finished," buy the plain one.

Some of you are in that 18%, and you know who you are. You like having the app, you'd use it, and I have no interest in arguing you out of it. We still sell both versions of most models, so the choice is yours. Mine is easy: on an air fryer, keep the $40–60; on a sous-vide cooker, get the Wi-Fi.
